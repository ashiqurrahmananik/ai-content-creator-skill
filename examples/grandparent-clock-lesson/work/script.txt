My grandfather fixed everything with a butter knife. The toaster. The garden gate. The radio that only played one song. People left him broken things and came back with working things, and he never once used a proper tool.

One summer, he put a stopped clock on the workbench and said, "Copy me."

I was nine. I thought this would be easy.

He tapped the back. I tapped the back. He squinted into the gears. I squinted so hard my cheeks hurt. He turned the clock upside down. I turned it upside down. He frowned at it. I frowned back. He nodded, as if I had said something clever.

Then he did the strange part. He closed his eyes, held the clock against his ear, and tapped one spot no bigger than a fingernail. I copied him. Nothing. He did it again. I did it again. Still nothing.

"Again," he said.

"I can't hear anything," I said.

"Again," he said.

So we did it again. Tap. Listen. Tap. Listen. The workshop smelled of dust and warm metal. Outside, the whole street was making summer noise — bikes, sprinklers, somebody's radio. Inside, there was only that small silence between us, and my grandfather's breathing.

His old friend Pell came in with a broken fan, saw our faces, and stopped in the doorway. "Lesson in progress," she whispered, and backed out again.

By the fourth try, my hand knew where to go before my brain did. Tap. Listen. Tap. The clock still didn't move. But I had started to hear things I had never noticed: the pipe in the wall, a fly at the window, his sleeve brushing the bench. The workshop was full of sounds. I just had to stop making my own.

I wanted to say it was stupid. I wanted to say the clock was broken and we should get a screwdriver. But every time I opened my mouth, he tapped that spot again, so I closed it.

Then he leaned close and whispered, "Now close your eyes."

I closed them. At first there was nothing at all. Then, from somewhere in the dark, a tiny tick. Not the clock in my hands. Something smaller, further away, steady as a heartbeat.

I opened my eyes so fast the bench seemed to jump. The clock in my hands was still stopped at four twelve. Still as a stone. The tick was coming from his pocket.

He was holding a second clock. He had been listening to it the whole time.

I stared at him. He shrugged, like I was the one who had missed something obvious.

"You were listening," he said. "That's the whole job."
